Start here

Quick start

Ammonite plays the moment it has power. Five minutes from here you will have changed its key, its tempo and its chords.

  1. Turn it down, then plug in. Turn knob 12 (VOLUME) to the left, connect the stereo output to your amp, mixer or speakers, and power Ammonite from USB. Then bring VOLUME up.
  2. Listen. Three arpeggios in A minor at 110 BPM. OSC 1 plays the low root and fifth in quarter notes, OSC 2 runs up and down the chord in eighths, OSC 3 picks random notes of the scale in sixteenths, leaving a few steps out. No effects are on yet.
  1. Change the tempo. Turn knob 6, TEMPO. The screen shows what you are turning and its value, then fades back to the rings.
  2. Change the key. Knob 4 is ROOT, knob 5 is SCALE. Try DORIAN or PENTA. All three arps follow at once; nothing can play a wrong note.
  3. Let the chords move. Turn knob 11 all the way right, to page 9, KEY. Turn knob 1 (PROG) to POP. Now the chord changes every bar, and every arp follows it.
  4. Add echoes and space. Knob 11 to page 7, DELAY: turn up knobs 7–9, the delay SEND of each oscillator. Then page 8, MIX: knobs 7–9 are the reverb sends.
Two things worth knowing now
  • Changing page never changes the sound. But when you then touch a knob, its setting jumps to where the knob points. The whole story is on p. 00.
  • Nothing is saved. Switch off and on, and Ammonite starts again from its power-on sound, with one exception: the page knob 11 points at when you switch on takes its settings from where its knobs are. With knob 11 on page 1, that means the levels, key, tempo and masters start where you left the knobs.
Twelve knobs, nine pages

The panel

Twelve knobs control about a hundred settings. They are organised so that once you know four rules, you can find anything.

2. Columns are oscillators

On pages 2 to 8, each column of knobs belongs to one oscillator, in both groups: knobs 1 4 7 are OSC 1, 2 5 8 are OSC 2, 3 6 9 are OSC 3. Each row does one job for all three: on page 5, for example, knobs 1–3 are the three CUTOFFs, 4–6 the three RESOs and 7–9 the three TYPEs. The screen lists a page the same way: 1-3 CUTOFF, 4-6 RESO…

Pages 1 MAIN and 9 KEY are different: they set things for the whole instrument, so every knob there has its own job.

3. Knob 10 is shared, or picks a section

On most pages knob 10 is one setting shared by all three oscillators (DETUNE, DRIVE, SIZE…). On four pages there is more to control than nine knobs can hold, so the page is split into sections and knob 10 chooses one: ARP, ENVELOPE and DELAY call it SECTION, LFO calls it TARGET. Choosing a section only changes what knobs 1–9 control. It never changes the sound.

4. A knob takes over when you turn it

Every knob on every page and section remembers its own setting. When you change page (or section) the knobs themselves stay where they are, so most of them now point somewhere other than the setting they control. Ammonite leaves every setting alone until you turn its knob. The moment you do, the setting jumps to where the knob points, and from then on follows it.

So glance at a knob's pointer before you turn it. If it points far from where the sound is, expect a jump. A tiny wobble does not count as a turn, so brushing a knob does nothing.

The screen tells you what you touched

About 2.5 seconds after you let go, the text fades and the rings return. Under a section map, the bright dot is the section you are in. On the LFO page, a colored dot marks a target that is doing something (its DEPTH is up on some oscillator) and a grey dot one that is off.

The ideas behind the knobs

How Ammonite plays

Three voices, one key and one clock. Everything on the nine pages is a detail of one of these three.

Three voices

Ammonite has three oscillators, OSC 1 to OSC 3. Each is a complete little synth with its own arpeggiator, envelopes, filter, LFOs, echo and place in the stereo mix. They play one note at a time each, so together they make three-part music. Inside, every oscillator is really two oscillators, pulled slightly apart by DETUNE for a fuller sound.

One key

You never choose notes on Ammonite. You choose a key (ROOT and SCALE on page 1), a chord within it, and then, for each oscillator, which of the chord's notes it may use. That set of notes is the oscillator's pool, and its arp plays from nothing else. Here is how the power-on sound gets its notes:

DEGREE (page 2) moves a pool's starting note up the scale from the chord's root: OSC 2 starts two scale steps up, on C, so its TRIAD is C E G. POOL and RANGE (page 3) say which notes to take and over how many octaves; OCTAVE (page 2) places the pool high or low. The colored dots are each note's screen color (p. 00).

When the chord changes, from a progression or from CHORD on page 9, every pool is rebuilt on the new chord, and all three oscillators move with it. SCALE can also be a chord type (MAJ, MIN7, ADD9…) instead of a scale: then the key is that chord, and a progression moves it around, keeping its color.

One clock

A single master clock (TEMPO on page 1) drives all three arps, the progression, the delay times and, if you want, the LFOs. Each arp divides the beat its own way (DIVISION), but every grid starts from the same beat, so however strange the combination, the three never drift apart.

Two bars of the power-on patterns. Each block is a step; rust marks the first step of a pattern. OSC 3's pattern is 6 steps long, so it starts over at different places in each bar; its DENSITY of 4/6 leaves out the same two steps every time round (hollow).

Three things shape the rhythm beyond DIVISION. LENGTH sets how many steps a pattern has before it starts over; different lengths make patterns that slide against each other. DENSITY leaves steps out, spread as evenly as possible. SWING (page 1, knob 10) delays every second step. And RESTART on page 9 can pull every pattern back to its first step on each bar or each chord.

Arps and drones

Set an oscillator's MODE (page 3) to OFF and it stops arpeggiating: it holds a single note (the first of its pool) and slides to the new note when the chord changes. A drone ignores the amp envelope's decay: it fades in over ATTACK and then simply holds. One drone under two arps, or three drones and no arps at all, are both good ways to play Ammonite.

ARP

Which notes each oscillator plays, when it plays them, and how often it surprises you.

Every oscillator has its own arpeggiator. On each step it takes the next note from its pool (p. 00), a handful of notes built from the key and the chord playing now. All three arps run from one master clock, so however different their rhythms, they never drift apart.

There are too many settings for one page of knobs, so ARP has three sections. Knob 10 picks the section. Choosing a section only changes what knobs 1–9 control, never the sound.

  • Patterns that drift. Set DIVISION to 1/8 on all three oscillators and LENGTH to 8, 7 and 5. The three patterns only line up again after 280 steps.
  • A little life. On the fastest oscillator, raise VARY to about 20 and RATCHET to about 10.

The screen

Reading the rings

The screen shows what the three arps are playing, as it happens. Once you can read it, you can see a pattern before you hear it.

  1. OSC 1 is the outer ring.
  2. OSC 2 is the middle ring.
  3. OSC 3 is the inner ring. Each ring is cut into as many arcs as its oscillator's LENGTH, starting at 12 o'clock and running clockwise. One arc is one step.
  4. The playing step is the big dot with a halo. It moves on one arc with every step.
  5. A rest is a hollow dot on a grey arc: a step that DENSITY leaves out.
  6. The line is the sound itself. It is the oscillator's real waveform: it swells when a note starts and flattens as it fades, and higher notes fit more waves round the ring. A drone is one unbroken ring.
  7. The chord playing now, in the color of its root note.
  8. Its numeral in the key. Lowercase means minor. When a progression runs, its place in it follows, e.g. 3/4.
  9. The beat: one dot for each beat of the bar.

Colors are notes

Every note has its own color, the same in every octave and on every view. The colors run around the circle of fifths: notes a fifth apart get neighboring colors.

That puts notes that sound good together close in color. A root and its fifth (the two notes of a FIFTHS pool) always look alike, and each chord of a progression brings its own mix of colors to the screen.

The wheel is drawn on black because the brightest colors, yellow and cyan, vanish on white paper. On the screen they glow.

Four views page 9 KEY, knob 10 VIEW

The same music can be drawn four ways. Pick one with VIEW; the readout and page maps look the same on all of them.

PITCH power-on

The rings, with each whole ring in the color of the note its oscillator is playing right now. As the arp moves, the ring changes color: you watch the melody.

RINGS

Each arc keeps the color of the note on its step, so the whole pattern is on screen at once, and you see it change when the chord does.

WHEEL

The twelve notes around the circle of fifths, C at the top. The chord's notes are joined into a shape: its outline shows the kind of chord. Each oscillator's note is a bright dot (biggest for OSC 1), nearer the rim the higher it plays.

SCOPE

The three waveforms drawn over each other, each in its note's color. The plainest view, and the best for hearing what SHAPE and the filter do.

Chord keys

Past BLUES, SCALE turns into a chord. Then POOL KEY plays that chord's notes, and a progression moves the chord through its parent scale (natural minor for MIN, MIN7 and DIM; major for the rest), keeping its quality: a MIN7 key gives a progression of minor sevenths.
